MORE SCHOOL ACCOMMODATION.

A $5,000 Addition to be Made to Barton
Street (East Hamilton) School.

A meeting of the ratepayers of East
Hamilton (School Section No. 7) was held
last night for the purpose of considering
the subject of enlarging the present school-
house, by adding two more class-rooms on
the ground floor, and putting a second
story on the present building. Mr. Frank
Squibb presided.

Mr. Rutherford presented the plans of
additions and alterations to cost between
$8,000 and $9,000. A discussion followed,
in which Messrs. Kinrade, Eastwood,
McNair, Shenton, Murton, Webster,
Burkholder and the trustees took part.

It was moved by Mr. J. W. Murton,
seconded by Mr. McAulay, That the
school trustees be empowered to afford the
additional accommodation required by
erecting a school south of King street.—

Professor Lugger was called to Otter Tail
County, Red River Valley, about 120 miles
from the Manitoba border. There he
found immense numbers of Rocky Moun-
tain locusts hatching out and nearly ready
to do great damage. Further inquiry re-
vealed the fact that for nearly 100 square
miles in that vicinity the pests were hatch-
ing. Dr. Lugger immediately appealed to
the Governor for State, aid, and mean-
while the farmers were organized, and the
tradespeople in the towns induced to shut
up their places of business for half
the day, ‘so great were the fears
that unless the utmost vigilance
were exercised the entire crop—the back-
bone of the State—would be devoured by
the voracious insects. The State aid con-
sisted of several hundred barrels of kero-
sene and tar, besides sheet-iron and other
supplies, but it did not arrive in time to
prevent the destructive army beginning its
work, and temporary arrangements had to
be made. Under the direction of Dr.
Lugger, a ditch two feet deep and two feet
wide was dug immediately in front of the
advancing host, which had come up in
armies from every field that had not been
ploughed for this year's wheat crop.
Every green thing disappeared before
the line of march thus formed, ex-
tending for many miles. Into the
ditch thus formed, a few inches of straw
was placed, and thelocusts were driven into
it by walking behind them. They cannot
jump out, Dr. Lugger said recently, and
are burned; or, if straw is not to be had,
they are killed by drawing a log through
the ditch. The tar is used by placing it
into a shallow sheet-iron pan two feet wide
and eight feet long, with a wide board
fastened to one side. This is drawn side-
wise across the field, the hoppers jumping
against the board and falling into the tar,
where they perish. But the handier, more
rapid and most complete method is to use
kerosene on canvas, against which the
pests jump. Strong muslin or canvas, a
yard wide and fifteenfeet long, is stretched
on a frame and carried on a sled-like
arrangement pulled by a team. The canvas
slants back, and is constantly saturated
with kerosene. Every one that hops
against this and touches his body to the
oil dies instantly. One barrel of kerosene
will go over about 120 acres, and will kill
200 bushels or more.

As in the case of many another pest,
there are other agencies besides these
artificial ones, by means of which the
grasshoppers are destroyed. Along with
them, in great numbers, come numerous
enemies, in insect form, prominent among
which is a red bug that is already destroy-
ing the hoppers in a wholesale way. These
bugs lay their eggs on the wings and legs
of the larger insect, and the young subse-
quently hatched make short work of the
major pests. Dr. Lugger, from his recent
investigation, looks to this agency of
destruction with much confidence, and
hopes to keep the invaders under control
though he does not expect to eradicate
them. He warns the farmers that they
must plough their fields this fall, if they

(3.) In the vast majority of cases, if the
County Council supply the gravel, the
statute labor will suffice to keep the roads
in repair; but a superintendent and 20
men could be kept at work upon the high-
ways for 300 days each year for less than
$7,000, which is an amount barely equal to
that now payable to toll-gate keepers and
for necessary expenses in connection with
the collection of the tolls, to say nothing of
the profits made by the road companies and

large portion of the winter these men, if
employed at all, could be at work preparing
material for new roads in portions of the
county not now supplied with stone or
gravel roads.

(5.) In this matter of roads, it is surely
proper that the Grange regulation, ruling

out middlemen, should be applied. The
people should have the roads without any
boundabout expense dickering with capi-
talists—there should be no second or inter-
mediary party to make a profit out of the
highways ; in short, the people should own
and operate the roads, and get whatever
profit there is in the process.

(6.) A frontage tax is now paid by all
who live on the stone or macadamized
roads, as property is assessed as more valu-
able there, and taxed proportionately ; but
this frontage tax does not go to support
the roads, as in equity it should, but to
raise the average value of those portions of
the county or township remote from the
roads. Why should this anomaly exist?
In levying taxes for the jail and court
house, a frontage tax is never dreamed of.

low. To the progressive farmer the con-
stant payment of heavy tolls is a serious
matter.

troubled with the Rooky Mountain locust,
but in 1871, when the last great visitation
took place, the insects spread in immense
numbers to Manitoba, and ate up every
living product of the soil in a wide district
of that young colony. At that time the
methods of combatting the hoppers were of
the most primitive character, and they had
pretty much their own way. Now, as Dr.
Lugger has proved, it is possible to combat
the plague even when its operations are
widespread, and there is little danger of the
unwelcome visitors finding their way
across the border. On thisfact our North-
west settlers are doubtless felicitating them-
selves.

A County Case.

John Connors, a resident of Barton Town-
ship, was before the Police Magistrate this
morning, charged by Hannah A. Connors,
his better-half, with using indecent and
profane language and threatening to kill
her and her family. The complainant said
the charge was true in every respect, while
Connors said if the woman lived till he
struck her she would live for ever. He also
said that he had made all his property over
to her and had not had any peace since
doing so. He had no witnesses and was
fined $2 and $7 costs.

forward the excuse that he did not know
anything about the circulation of bad
whiskey by his agents in the recent elec-
tion ; that he did not know about the crisp
dollar bills which the same worthies went
scattering around poor men whose integrity
they thought it possible to swerve by
temptation, and that he and Beaumont of
Glen Williams did not arrange for the pay.
ment of voters. Innocent Mr. Henderson 1
It was your wicked partners who did it,
was it ? Yet all the time, until you found
you could brazen it out no longer, you were
willing to take advantage of whatever
benefit was to be derived from the poison-
ing of voters by party whiskey of the most

you are a match for Montague, the con-
cocter of the forged address from the

A JUNIOR JUDGE FOR WENTWORTH.

Mr. John Muir, of the firm of Crerar &
Muir, has been appointed Junior Judge of
the County of Wentworth. In justification
of the appointment of a Junior Judge it
may be said that the amount of legal busi-
ness to be done in Wentworth (including
Hamilton) is much greater than that which
has to be settled in other counties which
have Junior Judges. The TIMES cannot
speak with approval of the choice made by
the Government, though Mr. Muir’s inti-
mate friends describe him as the possessor
of a “judicial mind." The proof of the
pudding is in the eating.
