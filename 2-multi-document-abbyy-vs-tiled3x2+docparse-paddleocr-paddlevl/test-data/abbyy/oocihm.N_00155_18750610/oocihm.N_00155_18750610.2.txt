Rules for the Care of the Eyes.

By Dr. D. F. Lincoln, Secretary of the Depart-
ment of Health, A.S.S.A.

When writing, reading, drawing, sewing,
etc., always take care that

. (a.) The room is comfortably cool, and the
foot warm;

(b.) There is nothing tight about the neck;

(c.) There is plenty of light without daz-
zling the eyes ;

(d.) The sun does not shine directly on the
object we are at work upon;

(e.) The light does not e né from in front!
it is best when it or des over the left
shoulder ;

(f.) The head is not very much bent over
the work ;

(g.) The page is nearly p erpendicular to the
line of sight: that is, that the eye is nearly
opposite the middle of the page, for an ob-
ject held slanting is not seen so clearly;

(h.) That the page, or other object, is not
less than 15 inches from the eye.

In any case, avoid fine needle-work, draw-
ing of fine maps, and all such work, except
for very short tasks, not exceeding half an
hour each, and in the morning, when the
eyes have any defect.

Never study or write before breakfast by
candlelight.

Do not lie down when reading.

If your eyes are aching from tire light, from
looking at the snow, from over-work or other
causes, a pair of colored glasses may be ad-
vised, to be used for a while. Light blue or
grayish blue is the best shade, but these
glasses are likely to be abused, and usually
are not to be worn except under medical ad-
vice. Almost all those persons who continue
to wear colored glasses, having perhaps re-
ceived advice to wear them from medical
men, would be better without them. Travel-
ling venders of spectacles are not to be
trusted ; their wares are apt to be recom-
mended as ignorantly and indiscriminately as
in the times of the " Vicar of Wakefield."

If you have to hold the pages of Harper’s
Magazine nearer than 15 inches in order to
read it easily, it is probable that you are quite
near-sighted. If you have to hold it two or
three feet away before you see easily, you are
probably far-sighted. In either case, Hit is
very desirable to consult a physician before
getting a pair of glasses, for a misfit may in-
jure your eyes.

Never play tricks with the eyes, as squint-
ing or rolling them.

The eyes are often troublesome when the
stomach is out of order.

Avoid reading or sewing by twilight, or
when debilitated by recent illness, especially
fever, or, in the ease of women, by child-
birth.

Every seamstress ought to have a cutting-
out-table to place her work on such a plane
with reference to the line of vision as to
make it possible to exercise a close scrutiny
without bending the head or the figure much
forward.

Usually, except for aged persons or chronic
invalids, the winter temperature in work-
rooms ought notto exceed 60 ° or 65 9. To
sit with impunity in a room at a lower tem-
perature some added clothing will be neces-
sary. The feet of a student or seamstress
should be kept comfortably warm while tasks
are being done. Slippers are bad. In winter
the temperature of the lower part of the room
is apt to be 10 9 or 15 P lower than that of
the upper.

task now and then, take a few deep inspira-

The •mbra Murder.

In our last joue we gave some account of
the murder o Mr. R. S. Finlay, at his resi-
dence in th Township of Sombra on the
morning e Saturday. From the devolep-
ments whch were made during the course of
the inquest, it would appear that there was
no suspicion at first that he had been murder-
ed by the inmates of his own house, but that
themurder had been committed by some per-
sol or persons who had gone to the place for
the purpose of stealing the horses, and had
committed the murder when they found they
were discovered, in order to avoid detection.
It appears that both Mrs. Finlay and the man
Smith manifested great reluctance to attend
the funeral, and that this circumstance, un-
usual as it is in country places, on the part
of either male or female friends, led the
neighbors to suspect that they must have
known more about Mr. Finlay's death than
they wished to admit. In order to have
the mystery solved, Dr. Mott of Dresden,
formerly of Wyoming, and still a Coroner for
the County of Lambton, was telegraphed for.
On his arrival, he found the body in the posi-
tion in which it fell after the fatal shot was
fired. He at once summoned a jury, and
proceeded to make the necessary inquiry into
the cause of the poor man’s death. A post
mortem examination showed that deceased
had been shot just below the left car, the
charge passing directly through his head,
scattering his brains over the ceiling and
wall of the stable. The gun with which the
murder was committed lay near by, and the
padlock and key of the stable were still in his
hand, and the lantern a few feet from the
body, broken as if thrown from his hand as
he fell; but the nature and position of the
shot precluded the idea that he had taken his
own life.

Mrs. Finlay was the first witness examined by
the Coroner. She stated thather husband
was a native of Ireland, and was forty-three
years of age; they were married in the Town-
ship of Dover nine years ago, and had lived
in Sombra three years. He was in tolerable
health, but subject to fits of despondency,
and people would sometimes say to her he
was crazy. On the day previous to his death
he complained of weariness, and went to bed
about 9 o'clock, she retiring about an hour
later. About 1 o'clock in the morning Mr.
Finlay got up and went to the stable, to see
what was wrong with the horses, there being
a noise in the stable. Not returning soon,
and hearing more noise, she called the men,
who went to the stable and found him dead.
She stated, in reply to the Coroner, that they
had "mostly," lived agreeably ; but had
differed about living on the farm, she being op-
posed to farming.

Smith, the hired man, said he was born in
Ireland, but had been in Canada since 1861.
Had lived in Toronto three years ; went from
Toronto to London, where he tended bar for
his brother, who kept the Thomson House ;
had worked in a flax mill in Belmont for
about six months ; went from there to Ar-
kona and worked in a flax mill there ; had
known Mr. Finlay for three years ; had left
him last spring on account of a quarrel about
wages, but had returned to him at the in-
stance of his wife. He said Mr. Finlay abused
his wife and children ; but this was contra-
dicted by several other witnesses who had
lived with the family from time to time.

The impression seems to prevail in the
neighborhood that Mrs. Finlay was unfaith-
ful to her marriage vows, and that a criminal
intimacy had existed between her and Smith
for some time; and the admission she made
in her confession, to the effect that Smith
told her on the previous Friday that he had
laid a plan to murder Mr. Finlay, naturally
strengthens the suspicion that the plot had
been concocted between them ; otherwise she
certainly would have warned her husband of
it, and prevented him going to the stable on
Saturday morning. Her confession we gave
in our last issue ; and upon that, and the
other evidence taken, the jury rendered a
verdict of "wilful murder" against
William Henry Smith and Mrs. Finlay
as an accomplice thereto ; and they were com-
mitted to take their trial at the coming
Assizes here. Smith arrived by the J. C.
Clark on Friday forenoon, and Mrs. Finlay
in the afternoon, by a carriage, having ob-
jected to come by the boat, and the Coroner
having acceded to her request to be sent by
land.

This "* Finlay horror” has created intense
excitement in the county, especially in the
neighborhood where it took place, being in
many particulars a counterpart to the Nissouri
tragedy which occurred two or three years
ago, and prompted by the same causes.
The parties, moreover, are much alike, Mrs.
Finlay being in physical appearance and tem-
perament Very like Mrs. Campbell, while
Smith does not look like a man who would
commit such a dastardly cold-blooded deed.
Both, especially Mrs. Finlay, are said to have
felt intense remorse at the result, and it was
no doubt the accumulating horrors of her
situation that caused her to give way and
make the confession she did.—Samia Ob-

WEEDS.—A PRACTICAL HINT.—Cultivators
are gradually learning that there is a hard
and an easy way to do things. We have
often spoken of the importance of killing
weeds before they came up, or before they
have reached a tenth of an inch in height.
It is then done with the single stroke of a
steel rake if in the garden, or by the single
passage of a light harrow, on the field. It
is often hard to beat this truth into the heads
of laborers. We therefore tried it by mark-
ing the time required for each mode, by the
watch. A bed of lowers, containing eighty
square feet, is inked once a week, whether
the weeds appear or not. It requires four
minutes for eachraking ; and for the entire
months of May,June and July, forty-eight
minutes. This leaves the bed perfectly
clean and mellov, and the plants have the
best possible chance. If the ground is full
of foul seeds, tley will come up in warm,
moist growing weather in one week, and
once or twice this green points had just ap-
peared when the weekly raking was given. In
another bed of equal size, where this is omit-
ted, they came up in a week, and in two more
weeks are from three to six inches high-
requiring over one hour to clear all out by
hoe and hand. "his must be repeated every

The Manliest Man.

The manliest man of all the race

Whose heart is open as his face.

Puts forth his hand to help another,

‘Tis not the blood of kith and kin,

‘Tis not the color of the skin;

‘Tis the heart that beats within.

Which makes the man a man and brother.

His words are warm upon his lips.

His heart beats to his finger tips.
He is a friend and loyal neighbor ;

Sweet children kiss him on the way.

And women trust him, for they may.
He owes no debts he cannot pay ;

• He earns his bread with honest labor.

He lifts the fallen from the ground.
And puts his feet upon the round

Of dreaming Jacob’s starry ladder./

Which Efts him higher, day by day.

Towards the bright and heavenly way.

And farther from the tempter’s sway.
Which stingeth like the angry adder.

He strikes oppression to the dust.

He shares the blows aimed at the just.

He shrinks not from the post of danger.
And, in the thickest of the fight.

He battles bravely for the right.

For that is mightier than might.
Though cradled in a humble manger.

Hail to the manliest man ! he comes

Not with the sound of horns and drums.
Though grand as any duke, and grander ;

He dawns upon the world, and light

Dispels the weary gloom of night.
And ills, like bats and owls, take flight—

He’s greater than great Alexander.

Effect of Weather on Crops.

The farmer has not only to put in his

crops and harvest them ; he has to see that
they are put in well, so that a good harvest
may be realized.

and this embraces a wide

ning May 19.

ph

field, with many points to be considered.
Upon the faithful performance of all these is
dependent the great success of our best
farmers.-

The weather, in its great variety, is one of
these points to be considered, or rather is a
part that contains many points. There are
some plants, for instance, that require more
warmth andmore moisture than others, and
vice versa. Wheat, grass, potatoes and oats
thrive best in a cool, moist climate, and the
cabbage still more so. There are others of the
same nature. On the other hand, corn loves
the sun, with a moderate proportion of mois-
ture. Warm, showery weather has always
been found the best for this crop. The grape-
vine is eminently a plant of this kind, only it
requires less moisture. In this respect, it is
in direct contrast with the cabbage and with
the different berries. The gardener knows
well that some plants require more water
than others; some demand more heat. Then
again, other circumstances have an influence.
Trees and deep-rooted plants require less
moisture from the atmosphere, being supplied
from the depths of the soil. The hygrometric
condition of the atmosphere, which affects
the smaller and shallow-rooted vegetation so
much, has, comparatively, but little influence
on trees.

There is much more that might be men-
tioned that complicates the matter (and
farther increases the embarrassment of the
farmer), without embracing the different bonds
of latitude which constitute the zones, but
only confine myself to our own, and I may
say, very changeable, temperate climate.

But can these changes and extremes in con-
nection with our various vegetation be suc-
cessfully met? Can we in effect harmonize
these contradictory elements? It is done,
happily, to a great extent, but it requires dis-
crimination—a knowledge of all that applies
to the case, and that is much. There must
be close observation in extensive practice. A
man must make his own science here, as no
regular system covering the case is found in
books. The first requirement is the proper
condition of the soil, and that means a gen-
eral, good soil—that is, well balanced, drained
and in a mellow and tolerably fertile condi-
tion, varied otherwise as circumstances or
crops may require. Where there is deep
drainage, a deep, good sub-soil, with surface
to match, as in our large river bottoms and
land thoroughly prepared in similar condi
tion, the basis for achievement is laid. The
conditions for warmth and coolness, moisture
and dryness, and for sound, healthy growth,
are met. The severe effect of winter and
spring frost is lessened, excess of moisture
passes off, drouth is withstood, and other
good results are secured.

With this basis of a good soil there is little
difficulty to adjust the rest, as already much
is accomplished. To come right to the point
of the opposite requirements of the different
crops, such as wheat, which demands a cool,
moist condition, and corn, which requires
warmth, we need but refer each to its own
particular place in the season—wheat to the
fall and spring or early summer growth, corn
to occupy the hot summer months. So we
also put out our spring crops of oats and bar-
ley early, because they are hardy and suited
to the cool rather than the hot- months. It
is therefore recommended and practiced by
the best farmers to put out these and other
spring crops as early as the ground will
admit, and a drained soil will admit it
earlier than an undrained. We avoid sowing
spring grain in June. Still later is still
worse. Corn is put out late, so as to get the
benefit of the heat at the start—which is re-
quired—and the full heat of the summer, to
be ripened in the warmth of early autumn.

But there are other things to be consider-
ed. The summer may be a cool one: what
then shall be done with corn? In such a
case we select a black, low soil between
hills which reflect and concentrate the rays
of the sun, and keep off the cold winds. It
must be a cold season indeed that will not
grow a good crop in a deep, rich, well guard-
ed river. So, to avoid failure, we confine
our grain to the uplands, where it is com-
paratively cooler, yet seldom cold enough, on
a deep, rich, well-drained soil, to produce
even an approach to failure. We put our
grapes where we think wo can get the most
heat, on land inclining to the south or east.
But we do not get it there ; we get it on an
elevated, northern inclination, where the
vine faces the sûn all day, from his earliest
rising to his latest setting, which is earlier
and later than the valley sees him, and much
earlier than with a southern inclination,
where the hill, extending above the vine, is
so much a bar to the sun's early and
late appearance in the summer, when
the sun' rises north of east and sets
north of west, thus favoring a northern expo-
sure. I give this as my own experience, and
for many years. Immunity from excessive
wet is difficult to secure; we must take our
risk with the climate, which, fortunately, is
dry rather than moist during the hot sum-
mer months and this is favorable—a'general
drouth suits the grape. We can supply the
lack of soil-moisture by attention to the land.
Thorough and deep drainage—in other
words, porosity—will do this. A cellar is an
excellent drain. This, in addition to surface
cultivation and mulching, will afford sufficient
moisture even in a severe drouth to grow a
good crop ; and such a soil will readily draw
off all surplus water. There will be no pro-
longed humidity from evaporation, as where
water is kept in excess in the soil near the
surface.

The effect of drouth on hoed crops, and the
garden, can be much mitigated and almost
overcome by a continued stirring of the soil
till the crop shades it and prevents further
working. Here both moisture and fertility
are largely added—much more than is usually
supposed, as only very few make the work
sufficiently thorough and continuous. Capil-
lary moisture from below and condensed
moisture from the atmosphere at the surface
are obtained by mixing air with the soil in
stirring and in its porous condition. The
ground and the atmosphere are the two great
reservoirs of water, holding a superabundance
at all times. These two sources must be
drawn upon in a drouth, and by the means
indicated, and not the surplus water in
streams and lakes. (This last comes under
the head of irrigation, and must be considered
by itself.)

From all this, and much more that might
be said, it will be seen there is nothing new in
the means required for meeting the irregulari-
ties of the season ; it is only the known prin-
ciples of agriculture, known to the best tillers
of the soil, and more or less carried out by
them. And this is well; for not only the ef-
fect of the weather is thus measurably con-
trolled, but the same means that require this,
answer also the purpose of general farming;
it is simply the thorough improvement of the
soil with a view to adaptability to the produc-
tion of the various crops, making the farm a
machine for the purpose desired. It pays to
do this, and it will soon be the only way that
will pay. The sooner, therefore, that the
farmer avails himself of all the advantages,
the better.—F. G. in the Country Gentleman.

A COSTLY PRESENT TO THE CZAR OF RUSSIA.
—The Czar will soon receive a magnificent
present from the Corporation of London, in
the form of a casket weighing nearly 70
ounces, and composed of the finest gold and
enamel. It is of the cinque-cento style,
oblong in form, 12 inches in length, 9 inches
in width, 12 inches in height, and supported
at the four corners by enamelled Imperial
Russian eagles, resting on malachite globes.
The reverse side of the casket bears the in-
scription : "To H. I. M. Alexander II., Em-
peror of All the Russias, from the Corpora-
tion of the City of London, 18th of May,
1874." On the obverse side of the casket is the
picture of the presentation of the address to the
Czar from drawings taken at the ceremony,
and executed in enamel on a plate of the
finest gold, 41 inches high in length and 21
inches in height. In this small space eleven
highly finished miniature portraits are exe-
cuted, all the dresses and decorations worn
on the occasion being accurately rendered.
The Emperor is standing upon the dais in
the Guildhall; on his right are the Duchess
of Edinburgh, the Princess of Wales and the
Grand Duke Alexis of Russia. On the left of
the Emperor are the Princess of Wales, the
Duke of Edinburgh, in Russian naval uni-
form, and the Duke of Cambridge, completing
the royal group. The Corporation is repre-
sented by its chief magistrate, the late Lord
Mayor, Sir Andrew Lusk, Bart., M. P., in
his state robe of ermine and scarlet, and Mr.
Russell Gurney, Q.C., M.P., the Recorder ;
the latter reading the address to the Em-
peror. Mr. Edward Hart, Chairman of the
Reception Committee, who proposed the ad-
dress in the Court of Common Council, stands
near the Recorder, wearing his blue gown of
office, and on his right Mr. Alderman Besley,
in scarlet robes. The end panels contain
views in enamel of the east and west windows
of the Guildhall, with the figures of Gog and
Magog. In the small side panels are the
arms of Sheriffs Sir J. H. Johnson, Sir
Charles Whetham, Alderman Besley and Mr.
Edward Hart. The arms of the Corporation,
supported by allegorical figures, artistically
modeled and chased, surmount the casket.

An ENAMELLED CELLING.—The London
Times says: Among the decorative worksin
majolica, mosaics, sgraffito, stained glass, &c.,
which were originated in the South Kensing-
ton Museum some years ago, when the late
Marquis of Salisbury and Lord Granville
were Lords President, but which have lately
been going on slowly or been actually sus-
pended, is a ceiling made of thin plates of
iron, and enamelled. This ceiling has just
been put up in its place in the central re-
freshment room, and is probably the first ex-
periment of the kind which has been attempt-
ed. The decorations of this room were de-
signed to resist all dirt and impurities inci-
dent to a public room where food is eaten by
an average of 10,000 persons a week. The
walls and columns are of majolica, the floor
is paved,and the ceilings are of iron enamelled.
The whole gives an impression of perfect
cleanliness, and every part might be washed
down by a fire-engine weekly, if necessary.
The iron ceiling was designed and painted by
Mr. James Gamble, a successor and pupil of
the late Godfrey Sykes, who practically
founded the school at the Museum. The
artistic work of the ceiling was done at the
South Kensington Museum, until the action
of the decorative studio was virtually sus-
pended. The manufacturing part of this
ceiling was done at Birmingham by the
Enamelled Iron Company, the white
enamelled plates being sent from Birming-
ham, and painted with charming and
vigorous arabesques by the artist. The work
is highly effective and the experiment suc-
cessful. In cases where it is necessary to
keep a ceiling clean and to wash it frequently,
this material promises to answer perfectly,
and the artistic work will last for centuries,
as the design is burnt into the enamel.

THE HOLYOKE DISASTER.-VERDICT OF THE
JURY.—The jury to inquire into the manner of
the Holyoke disaster place the responsibility
solely on those who constructed the galleries in
the church, which having a holding capacity of
400 persons, had no means of exit except one nar-
row stairway. They find that all the deaths oc-
curring from the disaster were of persons seated
in the galleries. They condemn those respon-
sible for the building of the church in the
strongest terms ; and further, find that the direct
cause of the fire, with its terrible sacrifice of hu-
man life, is attributable to the use of laces, paper,
etc., about the altar, and the fact that the build-
ing was sheathed with pine sheating, instead of
being plastered. They strongly deprecate the
use of such adornment, and the use of such finish
in places of public resort ; and, in conclusion,
admonish corporations, or persons owning, or
having in charge, public halls or churches, to
guard against the possibility of such a disaster
being repeated, assuring such that the public will
hold them to strict accountability.
