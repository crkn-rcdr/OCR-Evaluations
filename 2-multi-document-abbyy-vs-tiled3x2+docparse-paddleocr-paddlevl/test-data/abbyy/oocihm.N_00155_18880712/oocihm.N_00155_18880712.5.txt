Hamilton Produce Markets.

SATURDAY, July 7.—The market this morn-
ing was better supplied than it has been for &
good while. Vegetables were abundant and
small fruits were up to the average. Meats, too,
were plentiful. New potatoes sold at 40 cents &
peck and old ones at $1.50 per bag. Teas
brought 15c to 20c a peck; beans, 30c to 35c a
peck ; cabbages, 6c to 10c a head. Rhubarb, let-
tuce, onions and radishes were well supplied.
Strawberries were scarce at 10c to 124c a basket,
and cherries brought 7c to 10c a basket. There
were lots of imported watermelons on the mar-
ket selling at 25c and 30c each. Eggs sold at 20c
a dozen and butter at 22c a 1b. Lamb sold at
60c to 80c for forequarters and 75c to $1 for hind,
or 10c to 11c a 1b. by the carcase; mutton
brought 8c to 9c by the carcase ; veal, 4c to 5c a
lb., and beef 4c to 6c per 1b. by the quarter.
There were lots of flowers in, and the fish market
was well supplied at unchanged prices.

It is our intention to divide our sketch
into six letters, namely, take those who
settled about Sheffield, Clyde, Kirkwall,
Rockton, Copetown and Troy, each settle-
ment by itself, and in doing so may at
times have to retrace our steps in order to
pick up some important event that I may
have passed unnoticed, and while I shall |
do my best to get all the information that I
can and get it as accurate as possible, any
facts that my older friends may send me
will be thankfully received.

Beverly was always considered a poor
township ; so much so, with the exception
of a very few families who came in the early
history of this section of country, that the
neighboring townships were the favorites.
Beverly's rocks, its swamps and her tower-
ing pines stood out as a barrier against
her settlement, and to the emigrant who
was hunting a home in the west acted as
finger-boards, telling him, like the Wan-
dering Jew, to move onward ; hence it was
not until the year 1810 that that portion of
Beverly where Sheffield now stands was
anything but an unbroken forest. But in
that year the late John A. Cornell took up
the land where the most of Sheffield now
stands, the farm that he lived on being the
one now occupied by Mr. Christopher
Ricker, and he was for many years the
preacher, doctor and lawyer of the new
settlement. These professions are all
separated now, and it is thought thata
man can only do one thing at a time; but!
suppose thatin those early days the lawswere
fewer, the preaching more simple and fewer
people to get sick. However, about the same
time a number of other families settled in
the neighborhood, whose names we shall
mention, and many of whose descendants
live throughout this section, while others
have passed into oblivion and their names
are not known except by some of the older
residents. Another of the pioneers was
David Main, just east of the village, and
the farm is now occupied by his two sons,
Wesley and David. He had two brothers,
who came in about the same time, Adam
and Philip. Adam Main settled southeast
of Sheffield on the farm now occupied by
Joseph Ricker. The Mains were of Dutch
extraction and came from the State of New
Jersey. John A. Cornell had two brothers,
one named Joseph, who took
a plot of about 500 acres and
lived on what is known as the Heath
farm. I believe there are only two of his
family still living, Mrs. David Main and
Samuel Cornell. About the same year
another family by the name of Babcock
moved in and settled on the farm now
occupied by John Keachie, but after

• In 1834 the people began to feel the
necessity for some place of public worship,
and the present U. B. Church was built in
that year. I believe that it is the first
church which was built in the township of
Beverly. John A. Cornell was for many
years the minister. Ministers from other
denominations frequently preached in it,
and before the English Church, east of
Sheffield, was built, the late Dr. Boomer,
then of Galt, preached in it somewhat
regular. Dr. Miller, then of Galt (now
living in London, Eng.), would often drive
him down.

, —Mr. James Forbes, of Oneida Town-
ship, cut ten acres of timothy hay on
Saturday last in seven hours. The crop
is an average one, and one of the best hay
crops in this section.

—While Mr. Switzer, an old man, was
driving on a horse power belonging to
James Cowell’s drilling machine in Sen-
eca, he had the misfortune to get his feet
entangled in the machinery and badly
crushed.

—Mr. H. H. Dewart, President of the
Young Men's Liberal Club, Mr. R. Patch-
ing and Mr. B. Lynch visited Oakville
yesterday to make arrangements for seating
and other accommodation for the proposed
Young Liberals' demonstration.—Toronto
Globe. Though under the auspices of the
Young Liberals, old and young, male and
female, are invited to turn out and listen to
the addresses of the Liberal leaders. The
picnic takes place about the middle of
August.

—The Police Magistrate yesterday gave
judgment for $4 and costs against John
Duffield, of Glanford, for refusing to pay
wages to George W. Newman.

—Mr. William Ryckman, a farmer re-
siding in Louth township, was driving
home on a hay rake along the lake shore
road on Wednesday, when the horse ran
away and Mr. Ryckman received what it
is feared will prove fatal injuries.
