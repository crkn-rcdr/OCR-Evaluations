moisture from the atmosphere, being supplied

In our last ie we gave some account of
the murder oMr. R. S.Finlay, at his residence-
dence in th Township of Sombra on the
morning Saturday From the devole-
ments which were made during the course of
the inqrest would appear that there was
no suspicion at fi rst that he had been murder-
ed the inmates of his own house, but that
the murder had been committed by some per-
so or persons who had gone to the place for
fe purpose of stealing the horses, and had
committed the murder when they found they
were discovered, in order to avoid detection.
It appears that both Mrs. Finlay and the man
Smith manifested great reluctance to attend
the funeral this circumstance, un-
usual as it is in country places, on the part
of either male or female friends, led the
neighbors to suspect that they must have
known more about Mr. Finlay's death than
they wished to admit. In order to have
the mystery solved, Dr. Mott of Dresden,
formerly of Wyoming, and still a Coroner for
the County of Lambton, was telegraphed for.
On his arrival, he found the body in the position-
tion in which it fell after the fatal shot was
fired. He at once summoned a jury, and
proceeded to make the necessary inquiry into
the cause of the poor man's death. A post
mortem examination showed that deceased
had been shot just below the left ear, the
charge passing directly through his head,
scattering his brains over the ceiling and
wall of the stable. The gun with which the
murder was committed lay near by, and the
padlock and key of the stable were still in his
hand, and the lantern a few feet from the
body, broken as if thrown from his hand as
he fell; but the nature and position of the
shot precluded the idea that he had taken his
own life.
Mrs. Finlay was the first examined by
the Coroner. She stated that Her husband
was a native of Ireland, and was forty-three
years of age; they were married the Town-
ship of Dover nine years ago, and had lived
in Sombra three years. He was in tolerable
health, but subject to fits of despondency,
and people would sometimes say to her he
was crazy. Un the day previous to his death
he complained of weariness, and went to bed
about 9 o'clock, she retiring about an hour
later. About 1 o'clock in the morning Mr.
Finlay got and went to the stable, to see
what was wrong with the horses, there being
a noise in the stable. Not returning soon,
and hearing more noise, she called the men,
who went to the stable and found him dead.
She stated in reply to the Coroner, that they
had " mostly," lived agreeably; but had
differed about living on the farm, she being op-
posed to farming.
Smith, the hired man, said he was born in
Ireland, but had been in Canada since 1861.
Had lived in Toronto three years; went from
Toronto to London, where he tended bar for.
his brother, who kept the Thomson House; ;
had worked in & fax mill in Belmont for
about six months; went from there to Ar-
kona and worked in & flax mill there; had
known Mr. Finlay for three years; had left
him last spring on account of a quarrel about
wages, but had returned to him at the in-
stance of his wife. He said Mr. Finlay abused
his wife and children; but this was contra-
dicted by several other witnesses who had
lived with the family from time to time.

highly finished miniature portraits are exe-
cuted, all the dresses and decorations worn
on the occasion being accurately rendered.
The Emperor is standing upon the dais in
the Guildhall; on his right are the Duchess
of Edinburgh, the Princess of Wales and the
Grand Duke Alexis of Russia. On the left of

sented by its chief magistrate, the late Lord

1668, where his visit to Oxford is recorded,
he tells how he went to Brasenose College,
and says, " in the cellar find the hand of

The suggestion is unlucky; surely Pepys did
not mean a living child, but a Child entitled
to a capital letter, after the manner of
"Childe Harold"and"Childe Waters. At
Hale in Lancashire there is a tradition of a
" Child of Hale," a giant whose figure may
be seen on a local signboard, who is said to
have lived about a generation before Pepys,
and who is further said to have been at first
small of stature, but to have been lengthened
in answer to prayer, as Alice in Wonderland
was without prayer. And in Hale church-
yard is the tomb of John Middleton, the
Child, who died early in the seven-
seventeenth century, on which is further graven,
without any grammatical relation to the rest
of the epitaph, the words," Nine Feet Three."."
This has been naturally taken to mean the
height of the giant; but the tomb is cer-
tainly not longer than the seven feet of
ground allotted to ordinary men. Sceptics
have therefore suggested that the inscription
must have meant something else, and that
the story of the giant may have grown out of
the inscription. Yet here is a " Child of
Hale," who was clearly well-known a little
later than the assigned date, and who had a
" long " hand, as a giant of the height of
nine feet three might reasonably be expected
to have. Only haw did the hand of a giant
or any other person buried in a church-yard
in Lancashire get into the cellar of Brase-
nose College? The subject is mysterious;
perhaps some of the correspondents of Notes
and Queries may know something about it.
Anyhow, Mr. Pepys certainly never dropped
two shillings into the living hand, long or
short, of " John Middleton the Child," who
died about the time of Mr. Pepys's birth.

Selection of Thrifty Stock.
SUGGESTIONS ABOUT BREEDING.
In fattening animals much depends on
having good, thrifty stock to begin with.
There is a great difference in this respect,
some animals eating heartily at all times,
while others are always dainty and poor
feeders, particularly among ordinary native
or grade cattle. Doubtless there is equal
difference in specimens of full-bred cattle,
and it is worth more attention than it usually
receives. A pedigree is a good thing, but
only as it indicates good feeding, fattening
or milking qualities. All signs fail some-
times, and the result of the most careful
breeding will occasionally be a degenerate
specimen, ill-favored and practically worth-
less for breeding purposes. Such animals
should be sent to the shambles at once, no
matter what the loss may be to fancy breed-
ers. This is one objection to the pedi-
gree system. The fact that an animal is
registered in a herd-book gives it a value of
hundreds, perhaps thousands, of dollars,
without much regard to its intrinsic merits.
Unless there be some rule of this kind, the
cupidity of breeders will naturally prevent the
careful selection which should prevail as
much after a herd-herd-book-book is opened as before.
Good breeds are originated by carefully weeding-
ing out inferior specimens. If this process
be not continued, the breed will degenerate-
It is an interesting question for buyers
whether defective animals from pedigree
stock are ever refused admission to the There-
book. There is such a thing as reversion to
the original type, and if this be not recog
nized, registries and pedigrees may bie used
to perpetuate and disseminate defects as well
as excellencies. Breeders and purchasers do
recognize this fact to extent, as is
shown by the different values fixed on ani-
mals from the same strain. In my judgment-
ment the intrinsic worth of the animal ought
to have greater influence in fixing its price,
and the pedigree less. This might not be so
profitable a rule for breeders, but it would be
better for the purchasers and the public.
As regards general thriftiness, much may
be done by proper treatment and feeding
while young. Even an old animal may, with
care, be taught to eat almost anything fit for
food. This is particularly important for cows.

is established, the more a cow eats the greater

THE HOLYOKE DISASTER.- VERDICT OF THE
JURY.- The jury to inquire into the manner of
the Holyoke disaster place the responsibility
solely on those who constructed the galleries in
the church, which having a holding capacity of
400 persons, had no means of exit except one nar-
row stairway. They find that all the deaths oc-
curring from the disaster were of persons seated
in the galleries. They condemn those responsible-
sible for the building of the church in the
strongest terms and further, find that the direct
cause of the fire, with its terrible sacrifice of hu-
man life, is attributable to the use flaces, paper,
etc., about the altar, and the fact that the build-
ing was sheathed with pine sheating, instead of
being plastered. They strongly deprecate the
use of such adornment, and the use of such finish
in places of public resort ; and, in conclusion,
admonish corporations, or persons owning, or
having in charge, public halls or churches. to
guard against the possibility of such a disaster
being repeated, assuring such that the public will
hold them to strict accountability.

SUPERIORITY OF Ducks OVER HENS
Eaas.— The following details are given as the
results of French experiments: An ordinary
hen's egg weighing 60.4 grammes was found
to have 7.2 gr.of shell and skin, and 53.7 gr.
of contents, the latter, therefore, forming
88.07 per cent. of the whole weight. A duck's
egg, weighing 59.8 grammes, had 7.7 gr. of
shell and skin, and 52.1 gr., or 87.12 per
cent. of contents. Analysis of 100 grammes
of the contents of hens gave 26.01 dry
residue, 1.03 ash, 11.27 fatty matter in the
form of sulphure of carbon. An equal
weight of the contents of ducks gave
28.89 dry residue,1.16 ash, 15.48 fatty mat-
0
ter, showing that ducks contain a
notably larger proportion of nutritive matter
than hens. Comparison was also made
of the productive powers of an equal number
of ducks and.hens. From January to Au-
gust three hens laid together 257 eggs; three
ducks in the same time laid 401 eggs. Be-
sides this, the ducks laid 215 more eggs in
autumn, whilst the hens were off the lay.
Altogether the three ducks produced 617 eggs
in the year, whilst the three hens laid 257
only.-Practical Magazine.

A reform Convention for the county of
Welland was held at Crowland on Saturday
afternoon. The Town Hall was crowded.
R. Coulter, Port Robinson, was Chairman,
and J. J. Sidey, of the Welland Tribune,
Secretary. Hon. J. G. Currie received the
unanimous nomination to be the Reform
candidate in the approaching election for the
Ontario Legislature, which he, being present,
accepted. The meeting was very enthusiastic-
tic, and there was a feeling of certainty of
electing Mr. Currie, by a large majority, Mr.
W.A.Thompson, M.P., was present, and ad-
dressed the meeting, cordially endorsing the
proceedings.

Among turfmen in England there is a dif-
ference of opinion as to the relative merits of
a first-class English race-horse and a horse
of pure Arab blood. Admiral Rous, a great
authority, one of the Jockey Club stewards,
believes in the superiority of the English
thoroughbred, and thinks that the improved
race-horse can give an allowance in weight of
28 pounds to the Arabian. The result of a
match which took place at the recent spring
meeting at Newmarket in England between
Avowal, a four-year-old one-third of
Arab and two-thirds of English blood-and
the race-horse, Mr. Fox, seven years old,
hardly sustains the Admiral's view. Avowal,
although allowed only ten pounds, and hav-
ing been in training but three weeks, won
easily. It is argued that this result shows
that the infusion of Arab blood palpably en-
hances the staying powers of the British
thoroughbred, but, on the other hand, it is
said that this match cannot be regarded as s
true test, as Mr. Fox is far from being one of
the best horses in England, having been
beaten badly by Lowlander in the Hunt Cup,
and Scamp in the Goodwood Stakes.
