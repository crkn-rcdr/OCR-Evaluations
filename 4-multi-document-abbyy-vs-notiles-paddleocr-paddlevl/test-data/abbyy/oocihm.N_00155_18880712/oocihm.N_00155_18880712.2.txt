THE ISSUE IN THE STATES.

• Clear Statement of the Differences
Between the Policies of the Two Great
Parties.

The Republican party has taken up the
challenge of the Democratic party, and a
clear and definite issue is presented to the
American voter by the contrasted plat-
forms. Let us state this issue in our own
words. There is a surplus in the treasury
of $125,000,000, and an annual increase
threatened of $60,000,000. If this accumu-
lation goes on, this country will soon be
involved in hopeless bankruptcy, because in
that case the money which commerce needs
will be locked up in the treasury vaults.
To protect the nation from this serious
menace two policies are proposed. The
Democratic party proposes to confine
appropriations of public money to such
sums as are necessary for an economical
administration of the Government- to
retain the tax on alcohol to modify
the tax on tobacco; and to reduce
the tax on imports by admitting raw
materials free of duty and by reducing taxes
on all articles of necessity. If this involves
some manufacturers in commercial
distress, the party will regard the indi-
vidual injury as counterbalanced by the
general good. The Republican party pro-
poses to abolish the tax on tobacco ; to
abolish also the tax on alcohol used in the
arts and manufactures, if necessary to do
away with the national tax on alcohol
altogether ; to retain the present tax on
imports substantially unchanged ; to retain
it, not because it is necessary for revenue,
but because it will foster and promote
American manufactures and keep up
wages ; and it proposes to accompany this
policy of taxation with one of liberal ap-
propriations, not only for immediate gov-
ernmental necessities, but for the construc-
tion of a navy and of coast fortifications,
for river and harbor improvements, for
national aid to public education, and for
pensions. With this explanation we put
the politics of the two parties in parallel
columns, to make apprehension of the dif-
ference between the two easier and clearer

LONDON, July 4.—The Pan-Presbyterian
Council opened yesterday, Lord Cairns pre-
siding. Mr. Matthews, of Quebec, pre-
sented a statistical report showing that
there were four million communicants,
equivalent to twenty million adherents.
Respecting missions, 60,000 communicants
had been gathered into the Presbyterian
Church from heathenism. Over 500 mem-
bers had been sent to preach the Gospel to
the heathen. The report was adopted.

Mr. Drury, of New York, read a paper on
the influence of elders and deacons in the
working of the Presbyterian system.

Principal Cavan, of Toronto, read a paper
on the importance of fostering the spiritual
life of congregations, dwelling upon the need
of Episcopal oversight by the Presbyterians,
whose duty and prerogative it was to exer-
cise it. He advocated greater use of laymen
in Church work.

At Thursday’s session of the Council,
Mr. Chambers, of New York, who pre-
sided, introduced the subject of the duty of
the Church toward the tendencies of the age
relating to faith and life. He said that
Christianity now, as heretofore, had to
strive against so-called philosophy. The
disciples of Christ always lived in faith.
Still he believed that the gospel would go
on conquering.

Mr. Ellinwood, of New York, spoke of
the duty of the Church toward the specula-
tive tendencies of the age. The whole his-
tory of Presbyterianism was associated with
educational progress and with the defence
of the faith as delivered to the saints. The
forms of unbelief had been legion, but
Christianity was not beleaguered. The day
was coming when revelation and science
would be harmonized, because they would
be more fully understood.

Latest From the Northwest.

WINNIPEG, July 5.—For some time past
settlers in the vicinity of Stonewall have
been frightened by the appearance at fre-
quent intervals of a wild-looking man.
Children were afraid to go out of their
houses to play. Several attempts were
made to approach the man, but he for some
time eluded the fleetest runners and made
for the woods, hiding among the under-
brush. He was at last driven to bay, and
was brought into the city yesterday morn-
ing and given in charge of the Provincial
police. The unfortunate creature is evi-
dently insane, and presented a wild appear-
ance when brought before Magistrate
Moore at the Provincial Court. He had
scarcely any clothes, his hair was dishevelled,
and his face covered with a short wiry
beard. The man is unknown, and was re-
manded to jail for examination by the
surgeon._

Brantford News Notes.

BRANTFORD, July 5.—The unveiling of
the tablet presented by Bond Street Church,
Toronto, to Miss Clara Wilkes Currie, the
first Canadian Missionary to go to West
Africa, took place last evening in the Con-
gregational Church in this city. The cere-
mony was largely attended and was a most
impressive one. Rev. Dr. Wild, of
Toronto, performed the unveiling and
delivered a characteristic address, whilst
speeches were made by Rev. Mr. Hay, of
Scotland, and Rev. George Fuller, of
Brantford. The tablet is a very handsome
one.

A young Indian named James Johnston
was committed to jail to-day for stealing
wood off the Indian Reservation.

criminal assault and convicted. He is to
receive his sentence on Saturday.

BRIDGING THE ST. LAWRENCE.

The Work of Building the Canada-
Atlantic Railway Bridge.

An Ottawa despatch says: The work of
constructing the projected Canada Atlan-
tic Railway bridge across the St. Lawrence,
connecting Coteau with Valleyfield, has
been commenced. The entire structure
will be completed in a year. The work is
in charge of Mr. George Mountain, chief
engineer of the road. The bridge will be a
steel structure and crosses two islands. It
will have a drawbridge in the north chan-
nel to enable steamboats to pass through.
The cost will be between $1,000,000 and
$1,200,000.________.	_________

Obituary.

LONDON, July 4.—Rev. Father Pender-
gast, a member of the Dominican Order,
aged 28 years, died suddenly last night from
strangulation resulting from throat disease.
Father Pendergast was the son of Mr. J.
Pendergast, of this city, and received his
early education in London. At the age of 19
he went to St. Joseph’s Convent, Somerset,
Ohio, to complete his studies, and four
years ago he was ordained a priest and he re-
turned home from Ohio on the 24th of May.
Here the rev. gentleman's health improved,
and he retired to rest last night compar-
atively well, but at 11.30 he summoned the
household, bid them adieu and in a few
minutes he was dead.

Painful Accident to Mr. Trow, M.P.

The public will be sorry to learn that a
painful accident occurred to Mr. Trow,
M. P.. on Monday. Anxious to spend part
of the holiday with his friends in Mitchell,
he arrived in the town by the noon train,
and after dinner was induced to visit the
driving park, where horse races were in
progress. In the park he was invited to a
seat on the judges’ stand, and while chat-
ting with those around him the plank on
which he and others were sitting slipped
off its support, and in falling Mr. Trow's
leg was caught under it and severely
bruised.—Stratford Beacon.

A Jockey’s Fatal Experiment.

A St. Thomas despatch says : David
Adams, an employee in Shaw’s livery
stable, Ridgetown, on the advice of one
Graveline, took five doses of epsom saltsin
succession to lighten his weight while act-
ing as jockey for Dr. Lumley in the Ridge-
town races. He was found on Tuesday
morning on the Benton House steps asleep
and overcome by the treatment, and has
since died.

Choked to Death,

Two Fingers Cut Off.

PAISLEY, July 4.—About 10.30 this morn-
ing not a few citizens were very sorry to
learn that while working with an edger in
his saw mill, Mr. James Stark, of this place,
had lost two of his fingers and a third
badly cut on his right hand. He has been
milling for many years, and has the respect
of not only the Paisley citizens but many
neighboring townsmen and millers, and in
his accident he has our deepest sympathy.
Owing to the old gentleman’s courage the
doctors say he is likely to do well.
